# Day 43 Interview Guide — Keys and Data Relationships

**Interview goal:** Explain record identity, choose keys based on source/business constraints, and demonstrate how relationships remain trustworthy.

## 1. What is a key, in plain language?

A key is an identifier used to distinguish a record from other records. Think of a library accession number: two books may have the same title, but each physical copy needs a reliable identifier. In a database, a key lets us identify a row and connect it to other rows.

A key must be unique within its defined scope. Always ask: unique in which table, source, tenant or business?

## 2. Natural key versus surrogate key

A **natural/business key** comes from the business or source, such as customer number, SKU or invoice number. It is useful for tracing data back to its origin, but it may change, be reused or be unique only within one source.

A **surrogate key** is generated by the warehouse and has no business meaning. It gives the warehouse a stable row identity and can distinguish multiple historical versions.

Example: CRM customer 501 and Loyalty customer 501 may receive warehouse keys 10001 and 10002 because source context differs.

**Strong answer:** "I retain the source identity for traceability and use a warehouse surrogate key where integration or historical versioning requires it. I do not assume source IDs are globally unique."

## 3. Why is a customer name not a key?

Names can repeat, change, be misspelled or be formatted differently. Two people may be named Asha. A name is descriptive, not a reliable identity contract.

## 4. What does a primary key guarantee?

A primary key uniquely identifies a row and cannot contain NULL. PostgreSQL enforces this through a primary-key constraint. A table has one primary-key constraint, which can consist of one or multiple columns.

It does not automatically guarantee that every business rule is correct. For example, a unique customer key does not prove the customer email is accurate.

## 5. What is a foreign key?

A foreign key requires a child value to match a referenced key in the parent table, when the constraint is enforced. It protects referential integrity.

Example: an order's customer_key must reference a customer dimension row. A foreign key does not prove that the customer is the right person for that order; business validation is still needed.

## 6. Explain a composite key.

A composite key uses multiple columns together. In order lines, order_id alone repeats because one order has multiple lines. line_number alone repeats across orders. The pair (order_id, line_number) identifies one line if the source contract guarantees it.

When joining, use **both** columns. Joining on order_id alone multiplies rows and can inflate amounts.

## 7. Candidate key and alternate key

A candidate key is a minimal column set that uniquely identifies a row. A table can have multiple candidates. One is selected as primary; other candidate keys may be enforced as UNIQUE alternate keys.

Example: employee_id and company_email might each be unique candidates. If employee_id is the primary key, company_email can be an alternate key—assuming the business guarantees email uniqueness and handles changes correctly.

## 8. Why add source_system to source IDs?

IDs are often scoped to an application. CRM 501 and Loyalty 501 are not necessarily the same person. A source identity is commonly represented as (source_system, source_customer_id).

If the organization wants to unify people across systems, use governed identity matching/crosswalk logic. Do not merge based only on equal IDs or names.

## 9. Why are surrogate keys important in Type 2 dimensions?

One customer business key can have many historical rows. Each version needs a unique surrogate key. Facts store the version key that was valid at event time.

For example:
- customer_id 501 remains the business identity;
- customer_key 1001 represents Hyderabad version;
- customer_key 1002 represents Bengaluru version.

The key is not just an implementation detail; it preserves historical attribution.

## 10. Explain one-to-one, one-to-many and many-to-many.

- **One-to-one:** one employee record maps to one employee profile under the model's rules.
- **One-to-many:** one customer can place many orders.
- **Many-to-many:** one student can attend many courses and each course can have many students.

Many-to-many is commonly represented with a bridge/junction table containing the keys from both entities. The pair may form the bridge's primary key; additional attributes such as enrollment date can be stored there.

## 11. What is an orphan record?

A child/fact row whose referenced parent/dimension key does not exist. In PostgreSQL, an enforced foreign key can prevent such inserts. In data platforms without enforced constraints, run an anti-join test and alert on unmatched keys.

## 12. Unknown member versus inferred member

An **unknown member** is a predefined placeholder representing an unresolved or unavailable dimension identity. It can have reason codes (not arrived, not applicable, invalid source key).

An **inferred member** is created from limited source information when a fact arrives first. It is later enriched when full dimension data arrives.

Both require a documented resolution process, monitoring and fact-key correction policy. A placeholder should not make unresolved data invisible.

## 13. Scenario: duplicate key found in an existing warehouse.

I would first determine whether the duplicate is in the source identity mapping, the surrogate key, or a downstream join. I would identify affected facts before changing dimension keys, repair under controlled conditions, rerun uniqueness/orphan tests, and reconcile affected counts and totals. Prevention could include constraints, deduplication and pipeline tests.

## 14. Scenario: fact arrives before customer dimension.

I would preserve its source business identity in staging and apply the agreed unknown/inferred-member policy. The fact should not be assigned to an unrelated customer. When the dimension arrives, resolve the correct surrogate key, update/reprocess the fact if required, and validate the correction.

## 15. Scenario: report totals doubled after joining order lines to customers.

Likely causes include:
- customer dimension key is duplicated;
- join uses an incomplete composite key;
- a many-to-many relationship was not modeled with a bridge;
- dimension history versions were joined by business key without effective-date resolution.

I would compare row counts before/after join, inspect key uniqueness and cardinality, and reconcile amount totals.

## 16. How do you validate a relationship?

I would check:
1. referenced parent key uniqueness;
2. child key nullability;
3. orphan count;
4. expected relationship cardinality;
5. row counts before and after join;
6. business correctness of the matching columns.

A join that executes is not proof that it is correct.

## 17. Explain your Warehouse Key Management lab.

"I kept source identity as source_system plus source_customer_id, generated warehouse customer keys, and used (order_id, line_number) as the order-line composite key. I enforced primary, unique and foreign-key constraints. I tested duplicate source identity, duplicate order line and invalid customer reference. I also wrote an orphan diagnostic and documented unknown-member handling."

**Evidence to mention:** two source rows sharing ID 501 but receiving different warehouse keys; a rejected duplicate; valid two-line order; rejected orphan; zero-row orphan check.

## 18. Interview traps

- Assuming every source ID is globally unique.
- Using names as identifiers.
- Generating a new surrogate key on every reload.
- Joining on only part of a composite key.
- Treating foreign keys as a complete data-quality solution.
- Ignoring dimension version keys in Type 2 joins.
- Fixing duplicate keys without checking downstream fact references.
- Using an unknown key without tracking or resolving it.

## 19. Practice questions with answers

**Q1.** Order 900 has lines 1 and 2. Is order_id a unique line key?  
**Answer:** No. (order_id, line_number) identifies each line.

**Q2.** CRM 501 and Loyalty 501 are the same numeric ID. Can we merge?  
**Answer:** Not without approved identity-resolution evidence.

**Q3.** Why retain source keys if surrogate keys exist?  
**Answer:** Traceability, reload matching and source reconciliation.

**Q4.** What can cause a join to inflate sales?  
**Answer:** Duplicate dimension rows, incomplete composite join, many-to-many join or incorrect SCD version matching.

**Q5.** What should happen to a fact with no customer match?  
**Answer:** Apply documented unknown/inferred policy, preserve source identity, monitor and resolve—not assign a random key.
